In a small apartment, cord clutter is more visible by default — there's less room for it to hide, and every wall and outlet is closer to somewhere you sit, sleep, or eat. Cord management here isn't optional the way it might feel in a bigger house. Here's a sane starting order.

## Step 1: Find the Worst Outlet

Walk your apartment and identify the single messiest outlet — usually the desk, the TV setup, or a nightstand with a lamp and charger sharing one plate. Fix that one first. Trying to tackle every outlet in the apartment at once usually means you finish none of them.

## Step 2: Consolidate Before You Hide

Before buying any cord management product, check whether you can simply reduce the number of cords. Multi-port chargers can often replace three or four separate wall adapters, and wireless chargers eliminate a cable entirely for compatible phones. Every cord you remove is one you don't have to manage.

## Step 3: Vertical Space Is Your Friend

Small apartments rarely have room for bulky floor-based cable solutions. Wall-mounted shelves with built-in cord routing, over-the-door hooks for chargers, and cable channels that run up a wall rather than across a floor all take advantage of space you're not using for anything else.

## Step 4: Fix the Outlet Itself

Once the bulk of the clutter is handled, the outlet plate is usually the last visible piece. A wall outlet cover with a built-in cavity is a small, renter-friendly upgrade — no rewiring, reuses existing screw holes — and it's disproportionately effective in a small space precisely because everything is more visible there.

## What Not to Overspend On

Skip elaborate cable-routing furniture and built-in solutions unless you own the unit — in a rental, the products that give you the most value per dollar are the ones that are fully removable: adhesive channels, clip-on organizers, and swap-in outlet covers you can put back to stock before you move.

## The Real Goal

In a small apartment, "good enough" cord management means nothing is visible from your main sitting or sleeping spot, and nothing is a tripping hazard. You don't need a magazine-worthy setup — you need the two or three worst spots handled, which is usually 80% of the visual improvement for 20% of the effort.
